\section{Introduction and Motivation}\label{sec:introduction}

An alert threshold decides, at each moment, whether an anomaly score is high enough
to raise an alert. On a non-stationary signal a fixed threshold eventually drifts out
of calibration, so a natural instinct is to let the threshold \emph{learn} from recent
data. This report is about a specific hazard in that instinct, and about making the
choice of what the threshold learns from an explicit, auditable decision.

\paragraph{A plain worked example: the one-way ratchet.}
Consider a threshold that recalibrates itself from a short buffer of recent scores but
--- crucially --- only admits a score when the \emph{current} threshold judged it
\emph{normal}. Take buffer length $L=3$ and use the largest buffered score as the next
threshold (a nearest-rank high quantile), starting at $\theta=10$. Every admitted score
is by construction at most the current threshold, so the buffer maximum, and hence the
next threshold, can only stay the same or fall --- never rise. The threshold therefore
\textbf{never increases}: over a long run it grinds downward toward the low end of the
normal band, and every small fluctuation above the sinking line becomes an alert. The
flood of alerts is not evidence of more anomalies; it is an artefact of a threshold that
learns
only from windows it already called normal.

\paragraph{Why this matters.}
The failure is structural, not statistical: it holds for \emph{any} score sequence,
buffer length and quantile, and we prove it in Section~\ref{sec:problem}
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ratchet}). The opposite reflex --- admit \emph{every} score
unconditionally --- fails the other way: a sustained fault is absorbed into the buffer
and quietly becomes the new normal, so the threshold rises to meet the fault and stops
alerting. The two reflexes bracket the real question of this project:

\begin{quote}
\emph{When should an alert threshold learn, and from which data --- when the data are
chosen neither by the threshold's own decisions nor unconditionally?}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Our stance.}
We keep one fixed anomaly scorer and treat recalibration as one \emph{action} in a
small decision-time policy that, at each window end, chooses exactly one of four
actions --- \textsc{alert}, \textsc{hold}, \textsc{defer}, \textsc{recalibrate} ---
using only information available at that time. Recalibration is guarded: it fires only
from label-free evidence admitted independently of the threshold's own decisions, and
only within a band anchored to the frozen calibration threshold. Every policy is replayed
on the \emph{identical} score trace, so any measured difference is attributable to the
policy, not to a change of scorer. We keep \emph{proved} (the ratchet), \emph{measured}
(the full arm set on two development streams and a synthetic family) and \emph{proposed}
(the controller's benefit on real benign regime changes) visibly apart throughout.

\paragraph{Scope of Phase-I.}
The measured evidence is a development comparison on two SKAB streams (one labelled
event each) plus a deterministic engineering family; it is not a held-out result, a
generalization claim, or a statement that any policy wins. The controller is implemented
here; its evaluation on real benign regime changes, and the falsifiable experiment that
could kill the direction, are predeclared in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Literature Survey}\label{sec:literature}

Our object of study is a \emph{decision-time action policy on a common score trace}.
One fixed, source-fit scorer produces one score per window on the SKAB development
streams; at each window end a policy chooses \textsc{alert}, \textsc{hold},
\textsc{defer} (charged to decision coverage) or \textsc{recalibrate} (refresh the
threshold from recent label-free data) using only causal information. We organize the
survey around five ingredients an objector will attack: (A) the four-action policy;
(B) the single common frozen score trace shared by all policies; (C) workload and
coverage reporting alongside event recall and delay; (D) the chronological
source-fit / calibration / replay split; (E) label-free runtime with labels joined
offline only.

\paragraph{Adaptive thresholds and drift-gated recalibration.}
A large body of work adapts the detector or threshold when it detects drift. DDADE
combines real-time drift monitoring with a generative branch and triggers incremental
learning or dynamic threshold adjustment~\citep{yang2026ddade}; ADAPTS classifies
drift type and reuses models from a bounded pool~\citep{li2026adapts}; CDDIA detects
drift in normal data, interprets it, and retrains the detector to cut false
positives~\citep{xu2024cddia}. These adapt \emph{model or detector state}. We freeze
one scorer and change only the \emph{action} at window end, so the recalibrate action
is one option among four rather than a detector update.

\paragraph{Abstention and rejection.}
Sun et al.\ model scores as piecewise-stationary streams and output
$\{0,1,\text{abstain}\}$ with confidence-sequence guarantees on false-positive and
false-negative rates~\citep{sun2024online}; this is the strongest single threat to a
\textsc{defer} action. Perini \& Davis reject uncertain points with a \emph{constant}
threshold on a stability metric and bound the rejection rate and expected
cost~\citep{perini2023rejection}. Sun abstains from a statistical decision about the
quantile on an assumed-i.i.d.\ stream; Perini \& Davis reject statically. Our
\textsc{defer} is an \emph{operational} action charged to decision coverage and
reported alongside episode workload and delay under drift. We implement Sun's
stationary Algorithm~1 as a literature baseline on our trace so the comparison is
measured, not argued.

\paragraph{Label-free selection and workload-constrained evaluation.}
SEAD selects among base detectors online without labels and emits its own combined
score~\citep{shah2025sead} --- so it changes the score, the opposite of ingredient (B).
Hu makes alert episodes and workload contracts the object of
evaluation~\citep{hu2026workload} (in press). We borrow the evaluation frame from Hu
but bring a \emph{controller}, not a new scoreboard; SEAD, if used, would sit upstream
as a scorer, with our policies applied to its frozen output. Dynamic alert suppression
in AIOps~\citep{bhukar2024dynamicxy} learns a suppression policy from historical
\emph{alerts}, not from a score trace with an explicit four-action set.

\paragraph{The already-published failure mode.}
The self-referential-admission failure our rolling baseline exhibits is stated in
FITNESS~\citep{sankararaman2022fitness}: ``MEMSTREAM discards a sample if it is
predicted as an anomaly at the time of scoring \ldots\ such techniques can fail to
distinguish between anomalies and abrupt changes,'' with a formal buffer version in
its Section~5.3. We therefore \emph{do not} claim the ratchet phenomenon or the
adaptivity--robustness trade-off as ours; we cite FITNESS for the general failure mode
and contribute the deterministic monotone-ratchet proposition specific to nearest-rank
quantile thresholds with decision-gated admission. Metric behaviour that motivates the
identical-trace design is established by VUS/TSB-AD, which show ranking metrics are
functions of the score trace~\citep{boniol2025vus, liu2024elephant}; conformal and
change-point thresholding provide adjacent context~\citep{xu2026conformalized,
hennhofer2026nonconform}.

\paragraph{Honest scope (closest-work defense).}
Among the nine closest methods we checked on the seven allowlisted platforms,
abstention, drift-triggered recalibration, label-free selection, workload-matched
evaluation and the failure of learning only from self-declared normal points
(FITNESS) are each already published; what we did not find, in the papers we checked,
is recalibration treated as a guarded decision-time action whose training data is
chosen neither by the threshold being updated nor unconditionally, compared with
alternatives on one fixed score trace with alert workload, decision coverage, delay
and event recall reported separately --- a bounded search result, not a claim that no
such method exists.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Setting and information boundary.}
A fixed, source-fit scorer emits one score $s_t$ per window at window end
$t=1,2,\dots$ (window length 4, stride 4, segment-start anchor, incomplete windows
dropped). All scoring and every decision are causal and use scores only; labels are
never read at runtime and are joined offline after predictions are saved. We define
symbols once and reuse them:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item $s_t$ --- the anomaly score of the window ending at $t$ (\emph{score trace}).
  \item $\theta_t$ --- the alert threshold in force when window $t$ is judged; an
    alert is raised iff $s_t > \theta_t$.
  \item $\theta_0$ --- the frozen calibration threshold: the nearest-rank $0.95$
    quantile of the calibration-window scores (\emph{anchor}).
  \item $R$ --- a rolling buffer of the last $L$ scores; $q(R)$ is its nearest-rank
    $0.95$ quantile ($\lceil 0.95\,|R|\rceil$-th smallest); $\mathrm{med}(R)$ its
    ordinary median.
  \item action set $\mathcal{A}=\{\textsc{alert},\textsc{hold},\textsc{defer},
    \textsc{recalibrate}\}$; a \textsc{defer} is charged to \emph{decision coverage}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What is measured.}
Per stream $\times$ arm we report, with denominators shown: decisions; alert windows;
alert episodes; event recall (num/den); episode precision; false episodes; mean delay
(sample-index units); total and non-event alert duration; in-event alerted fraction
(derived); episodes per 1{,}000 decisions; decision coverage (covered/total);
deferrals; recalibration count; and resource scope. \emph{Coverage} is the fraction of
windows given an alert-capable decision (not deferred); \emph{workload} is the alert
volume (episodes per 1{,}000 decisions and alert duration). Because every policy
replays the identical trace, ranking metrics are identical by construction and are not
reported per policy.

\paragraph{The self-referential ratchet.}
The rolling baseline admits a score to $R$ only when the current threshold judged it
normal, then sets the threshold to $q(R)$. We prove this can only fall.

\begin{proposition}[Monotone non-increasing self-referential threshold]\label{prop:ratchet}
Let a threshold be updated by: at each step, admit the current score $s$ to a buffer of
capacity $L\ge 1$ \emph{only if} $s\le\theta$ (the current threshold), drop the oldest
when over capacity, and set the new threshold to the nearest-rank $q$-quantile of the
buffer, $q\in(0,1]$. Then for every score sequence the threshold is non-increasing:
$\theta_{t+1}\le\theta_t$ for all $t$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $k=\lceil q\,|R|\rceil$ be the rank in use, so $\theta_t$ is the $k$-th smallest of
the buffer. Two cases. \emph{(i) The score is not admitted:} the buffer is unchanged,
so $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t$. \emph{(ii) The score $s$ is admitted:} admission requires
$s\le\theta_t$. Before insertion at least $k$ buffer elements are $\le\theta_t$
(because $\theta_t$ is the $k$-th smallest). Adding $s\le\theta_t$ gives at least $k+1$
elements $\le\theta_t$. Dropping the oldest element removes at most one, leaving at
least $k$ elements $\le\theta_t$; a buffer that has grown by one raises the rank
$k'=\lceil q\,|R'|\rceil$ by at most one, i.e.\ $k'\le k+1$. Hence among the (at least
$k$) elements $\le\theta_t$ the $k'$-th smallest is $\le\theta_t$, so
$\theta_{t+1}=q(R')\le\theta_t$. The empty-buffer start admits $s\le\theta_0$ and sets
$\theta=s\le\theta_0$. In all cases $\theta_{t+1}\le\theta_t$. \qed
\end{proof}

The proposition is a \emph{mechanism} result: it explains, without appeal to the data,
why the rolling baseline produces a large alert volume at the same event recall as the
fixed baseline. The unconditional variant ($\text{admit all}$) has no such bound and
tracks whatever arrives, including a sustained fault. Both motivate an admission rule
that is \emph{separated} from the threshold's own decisions and \emph{anchored} to
$\theta_0$.

\section{Objective and Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\paragraph{Objective.}
Determine whether separating a threshold's recalibration data from that threshold's own
decisions, under an anchored guard with explicit deferral, avoids both self-referential
collapse and unconditional fault absorption, and at what cost in coverage and delay ---
measured on one common score trace against every baseline.

\paragraph{Pipeline and chronological split.}
Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows the causal data flow. Each source stream is split
chronologically into source-fit, calibration and replay; the scorer is fit on the
source segment only; the fixed-quantile calibration produces $\theta_0$; one score
trace is replayed identically through every policy arm; predictions and a label-free
action log are saved; labels are joined offline to form episodes. The reserved lane
(\texttt{SKAB/other/21.csv}) is drawn locked and unused.

  \caption{Chronological split and causal data flow. The scorer is fit on the
  source segment only; a single score trace fans out to all policy arms; predictions

  \label{fig:flow}

\paragraph{The controller (implemented).}\Simplemented{}
The proposed controller, the class \texttt{Anchored}\discretionary{}{}{}\texttt{Recalibration}\discretionary{}{}{}\texttt{Policy}, has frozen parameters
$L=10$, $q=0.95$, cap $\kappa=4.0$, stability $\rho=2.0$, defer limit $D=10$. It keeps
a threshold $\theta$ (starting at $\theta_0$), a mode (\textsc{normal},
\textsc{suspect} or \textsc{escalated}), a defer counter $c$, and
a buffer $R$ of the last $L$ scores of \emph{every} window (untruncated --- admission
does not depend on any decision). Listing~\ref{lst:ctrl} transcribes the frozen
controller exactly. Figure~\ref{fig:controller} is its state machine.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=ctrl, caption={The frozen controller, transcribed exactly from the protocol. \texttt{quantile} is nearest rank; \texttt{median} is the ordinary median.}, label={lst:ctrl}, captionpos=b]
append s to R (drop oldest beyond L)
full     = |R| == L
elevated = full and median(R) > theta
if mode == escalated:
    if elevated: return ALERT if s > theta else HOLD    # stay escalated, no recalibration
    mode = normal                                       # elevation over; fall through
if elevated:
    cand   = quantile(R)
    stable = max(R) <= rho * median(R)
    if stable and cand <= kappa * theta0:
        theta = max(theta0, cand); mode = normal; c = 0 # action RECALIBRATE (up)
        return (ALERT if s > theta else normal), action=RECALIBRATE
    if c < D: c += 1; mode = suspect; return DEFER      # guard failed: withhold
    mode = escalated; c = 0; return ALERT if s > theta else HOLD
# not elevated
if mode == suspect: mode = normal; c = 0
if full and theta > theta0 and quantile(R) < theta:
    theta = max(theta0, quantile(R))                    # action RECALIBRATE (return to anchor)
return ALERT if s > theta else HOLD
\end{lstlisting}

  escalated, with the hold, alert, defer and recalibrate edges and their guard

\paragraph{Parameter provenance (no tuning).}
$L=10$ and $q=0.95$ are the existing rolling baseline's history length and quantile;

block may exceed twice the block median. $\kappa$ and $\rho$ are operating assumptions,
not estimates, and were not chosen by inspecting any SKAB outcome (on the development
streams the guard is never reached). Figure~\ref{fig:concept} is a schematic contrast

\section{Simulation Platform and Requirements}\label{sec:platform}

The platform is a Python 3.12 standard-library-only package: no third-party runtime
dependency, deterministic execution, and \texttt{unittest} for the test suite. The
pipeline exposes reusable loaders, chronological splits, a scoring interface, a
fixed-quantile calibration, the policy arms, and an offline evaluator; results are
saved as per-window predictions plus an action log, and every reported row is
recomputed by \texttt{recompute\_evaluations.py} before it enters this report.
Determinism is by construction (no randomness in scoring or the policies; the
randomized property test for H1 seeds its own generator). No GPU or special hardware is
required; the runs complete on a commodity CPU and the runner records elapsed seconds
and peak Python allocation as the resource scope.

\paragraph{Dataset and provenance limits.}\Smeasured{}
The development data are two SKAB streams, \texttt{valve1/0.csv} and
\texttt{valve2/0.csv}~\citep{skab2020}, scored univariately on the \texttt{Current}
channel. Dataset origin and licence are \emph{not} independently verified: valve1's
acquisition URL is unknown and its licence applicability is conditional, and valve2
provenance is carried from an earlier manifest (claim C20). Results are therefore
development-only. Each stream contains exactly one labelled event
(valve1 $[574,975)$, valve2 $[562,956)$), so recall is measured over one event per
stream. \texttt{SKAB/other/21.csv} is named as reserved in the manifest; its

\section{Results and Analysis}\label{sec:results}

All numbers below are read from the recomputed study
(\texttt{20260929-policy-study-v2}, independently recomputed at zero mismatches over 99

\subsection{The one-way ratchet (H1)}\label{sec:results-ratchet}
\Sproved{}\,\Smeasured{} The normal-only rolling threshold never rises: it records 0
increases in 143 (valve1) and 140 (valve2) replay steps and ends at $0.258$ (valve1,
from $1.309$) and $0.406$ (valve2, from $1.126$), well below the replay median score.
This is exactly what Proposition~\ref{prop:ratchet} predicts, and it mechanically
explains the rolling arm's large alert volume at the same single-event recall as fixed.
Figure~\ref{fig:ratchet} plots the two SKAB replay traces with the fixed anchor and both
rolling thresholds.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{One event per stream.} Each development stream has a single labelled event
covering roughly 70\% of replay decisions and starting at the first replay decision, so
``$1/1$ recall'' is nearly uninformative (E4) and \textbf{recall saturates} --- one
alert anywhere inside the long event scores full recall; workload, false episodes,
in-event alerted fraction, coverage and delay carry the information.
\textbf{Scorer limit, not policy limit} (E3): inside the event only a small fraction of
windows exceed the calibration threshold and exceedance runs never exceed two windows,
which is why the persistence baselines miss events --- a policy redistributes alerts but
cannot create separation the scorer lacks.
\textbf{Univariate scope.} Scoring uses the single \texttt{Current} channel only.
\textbf{Provenance} (C20): dataset origin and licence are not independently verified; all
results are development-only.
\textbf{Held-out boundary.} \texttt{SKAB/other/21.csv} is named as reserved in the
manifest; its reservation authority and recipe are disputed; it was never opened and no
claim is made.

\paragraph{Strongest opposing conclusion.}
The strongest honest reading is that these results do \emph{not} establish a useful
controller. On the benign cases the admit-all rolling rule has \emph{lower} workload than
the controller --- non-event alert duration $12$ vs $16$ on Y1 and $28$ vs $204$ on Y2 ---
so on workload alone admit-all beats it. The controller's case rests only on fault
retention: on Y3 its in-event alerted fraction is $0.29$ against admit-all's $0.04$ at
recall $1/1$ for both (admit-all barely alerts the fault it has absorbed), while fixed
alerts $1.000$ of the Y3 event at non-event alert duration $521$. On SKAB the controller is
\emph{indistinguishable from fixed} (identical rows on valve1 and valve2), so SKAB provides
no evidence for it; and Sun's abstention reaches low workload only by deciding on $54/143$
and $57/140$ SKAB windows --- buying quiet with coverage, not a better decision. A credible
Phase-I conclusion is therefore a reproducible negative / inconclusive development finding
plus a proved mechanism and a predeclared discriminating experiment --- not the
superiority of any policy.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We proved that normal-only rolling recalibration of a nearest-rank quantile threshold
is monotone non-increasing --- a one-way ratchet (Proposition~\ref{prop:ratchet}) ---
and measured its cost on two SKAB development streams: it recalls the same single event
as the fixed threshold at $16.2\times$ (valve1) and $3.9\times$ (valve2) the alert
windows. We implemented an admission-separated, anchored recalibration action with
explicit deferral and compared it with seven baselines and three ablations on identical
score traces. On both SKAB streams the controller behaves exactly like the fixed
threshold (the guard is never reached), so the SKAB result is a no-harm / regression
check that H2 passes with false episodes equal to fixed; no refutation condition fired
on SKAB. Under deterministic synthetic stress the mechanism behaves as designed and
fails exactly where any label-free rule must: it cuts non-event alert duration on the
benign step Y1 (16 vs fixed 597) while still alerting the post-shift faults on Y3 and Y7
(recall $1/1$), it locks into periodic alerts on the Y2 ramp, and it absorbs the Y4
fault-shaped-like-a-step after onset (the declared limit H4). Any benefit on \emph{real}
benign regime changes remains \emph{proposed} and under test; the synthetic family is an
engineering check, and on those benign cases the admit-all rule already achieves lower
workload than the controller (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{The falsifiable next experiment (predeclared).}
On SKAB streams admitted by a label-independent rule (every \texttt{valve1/*.csv} and
\texttt{valve2/*.csv} other than the two development files, with calibration ending at
the declared first labelled row), replay all arms on common traces, plus one public
stream family with labelled \emph{benign} regime changes. \textbf{Kill criteria for the
direction:} (a) on streams where the label-free elevation test fires, the controller
does not reduce non-event alert duration versus fixed at equal event recall; or (b) it
loses event recall on any stream where fixed has it; or (c) Sun's abstention matches it
at equal coverage. Any of these kills the claim and becomes the reported result.
